1. What We Are Building

SyncUp is a chief of staff for student organizations. An organizer describes an event in plain English inside their Discord server, and SyncUp does three things in one conversation: finds a time that actually works for the people who matter, reserves the equipment and room, and drafts a budget the org can afford.

The problem, precisely

Student orgs are run by volunteers with no shared calendar, no inventory system, and a budget that lives in a spreadsheet somebody's predecessor made. Three specific failures repeat every semester:

  • Scheduling by poll. Someone posts five options in a group chat, six people reply, three don't, and the meeting lands during an exam week anyway.
  • Resource collisions. Two committees book the same projector because nobody tracks it. This is discovered on the day.
  • Budget drift. Nobody knows the remaining semester balance until the treasurer reconciles at the end, by which point it's overspent.

The insight that makes it one product

All three failures are the same problem: an unmanaged constraint that only surfaces after commitment. A person is busy 7–9pm. A projector is booked 7–9pm. A budget line is spent. Each is a claim on a limited resource across an interval, and each collision is an overlap that a computer can detect before a human commits.

This is the pitch, and it's also the architecture. One conflict-detection primitive, three domains. That's why this reads as a coherent product built by three people rather than three hackathon demos stapled together — and it's the answer when a judge asks "why is this one app?"

3. Business Logic — Scheduling MEMBER A

Why "who is free" is the wrong question

Naive schedulers intersect everyone's free time and return the gaps. In a student org that returns nothing — with twelve members and real course loads, there is frequently no slot where all twelve are free. A tool that answers "no times available" is worse than the group chat it replaced.

So SyncUp asks a different question: which slot loses the least? Every candidate slot gets a score, and the organizer picks from ranked trade-offs rather than waiting for a perfect answer that does not exist.

Weighted attendance

Not every absence costs the same. A budget approval meeting without the treasurer is worthless; the same meeting without one general member is fine. Members carry a priority_weight:

RoleWeightReasoning
president3Meeting typically cannot proceed without them
exec / treasurer2Decision-makers for their domain
member1Valuable but substitutable

Attendance score is the weighted fraction of required attendees who are free, not the headcount. A slot where 8 of 12 are free but the president is missing scores below a slot where 7 of 12 are free and all exec are present. This one decision produces most of the "it actually understands our org" feeling in the demo.

Hard vs soft constraints

Some conflicts eliminate a slot; others merely cost it points. Getting this split right is what separates a useful tool from an annoying one.

TypeExamplesEffect
HardExam, timetabled class, explicit "must be before Friday"Slot is removed entirely
SoftPart-time shift, other club, late night, exam proximityPoint penalty; slot survives

The rationale: a student cannot skip an exam, but they might move a shift for something important. Treating a shift as hard silently throws away workable options; treating an exam as soft produces a suggestion that destroys the tool's credibility the moment a judge reads it.

The fairness problem

Pure optimization has an ugly failure mode: it will repeatedly pick the slot that excludes the same person, because that person's schedule is the awkward one. Over a semester, that member quietly stops being part of the org.

SyncUp tracks a fairness debt — how often each member has been the one excluded across recent events — and applies a growing penalty to slots that exclude someone already carrying debt. The system deliberately accepts a slightly worse slot to avoid systematically sidelining a person.

This is worth a sentence in the pitch. It shows the team thought about what optimization does to people, not just about the optimizer. It also costs about fifteen lines of code.

Why the LLM does not score

The model has exactly two jobs in this feature: turn a sentence into structured constraints, and turn a ranked list into a readable explanation. Every number is computed by deterministic Python.

  • Reproducibility. The same request twice gives the same answer. LLM scoring drifts, and drifting on stage is fatal.
  • Explainability. "Tuesday scores 82 because 10 of 12 weighted attendance minus 15 for late start" is auditable. "The AI thought Tuesday was good" is not.
  • Speed and cost. Scoring 300 candidate slots via an LLM is slow and burns free-tier quota. In Python it is microseconds.

4. Business Logic — Resource Planning MEMBER B

A reservation is an interval claim

The core model: a reservation claims N units of resource R over interval [start, end). Availability is total quantity minus the sum of overlapping claims. That single sentence covers a projector, 100 chairs, and a room — the code does not branch per item type.

Two resource kinds fall out of it naturally:

KindExampleRule
ExclusiveThe projector, a specific roomAny overlap is a conflict. Enforced by a PostgreSQL exclusion constraint
PooledChairs, microphones, tableclothsOverlap is fine until summed quantity exceeds stock. Checked in a serializable transaction

Why holds expire

When a packing list is generated, resources are placed on hold with a 30-minute TTL rather than being confirmed outright. This exists because of a real behaviour: an organizer opens the bot, gets a plan, then goes to dinner without pressing approve.

  • Confirming immediately would lock the projector for an event that never happens.
  • Not reserving at all creates a race — two organizers both see it as available and both approve.

The hold is a soft claim that decays. A background job sweeps expired holds every five minutes and releases them. This is standard practice in ticketing and hotel systems, and saying so out loud is a credibility signal in the pitch.

Why the double-booking guard lives in the database

Application-level checks — read availability, then insert — have a window between the read and the write where a second request can slip through. Under demo conditions with two people clicking at once, this reliably breaks.

PostgreSQL's btree_gist exclusion constraint makes the overlap rule a property of the data rather than a property of the code path. It is impossible to insert a conflicting exclusive reservation, regardless of what the application does. Concurrency correctness enforced by the schema is a genuinely strong technical detail for a 24-hour project.

What the LLM contributes, and its leash

The model infers a packing list from an event description — "orientation night, 100 people" becomes projector, 2 mics, sign-in table, 100 chairs. This is real value: it is judgment about the physical world that a rules engine cannot supply.

Its output is constrained hard:

  • The org's actual inventory is injected into the prompt as context, so it proposes things you plausibly own.
  • Output is a JSON schema validated by Pydantic — malformed responses are rejected, not patched.
  • Resource IDs are matched server-side by name. A hallucinated ID is impossible to act on because the model's IDs are never trusted.
  • Anything unmatched becomes an "unowned item" and flows to finance as a rental line, rather than erroring.

The pattern generalizes: the model proposes in an open vocabulary; the system resolves against a closed one.

5. Business Logic — Financial Planning MEMBER C

Three numbers, not one

The most common bug in budget software is treating "spent" as a single figure. SyncUp keeps three:

NumberMeaningSource
allocatedWhat the org was given this semesterSet once per semester
committedApproved for events that haven't happened yetSum of approved estimated_total
actualMoney genuinely out the doorSum of approved expenses

remaining = allocated − committed − actual. The committed figure is the one that matters and the one spreadsheets always miss: an org with $500 left and $450 already promised to next week's event does not have $500. Once an event completes, its committed amount is replaced by its actual spend so nothing is double-counted.

Why estimates are anchored to history

Asking a language model "what does catering for 100 students cost?" produces a plausible number from a general prior — which may be badly wrong for your campus, your currency, and your usual vendor.

So before drafting, SyncUp queries the org's own past line items for each category and injects those averages into the prompt. The model's job shifts from inventing prices to applying known prices to a new event's scale. This is why seeding four past events with realistic spend matters: it makes the second budget visibly smarter than the first, which is a story you can tell on stage.

The LLM never touches arithmetic

The model proposes line items — category, description, unit cost, quantity. Python computes every total using Decimal:

line_total      = unit_cost * quantity
subtotal        = sum(line_totals)
contingency     = subtotal * Decimal("0.10")
estimated_total = subtotal + contingency

Two reasons. First, models make arithmetic errors, and a budget whose column does not add up is the single most damaging thing a judge can notice. Second, currency in binary floating point produces drift — NUMERIC(12,2) in PostgreSQL and Decimal in Python, never float, anywhere.

The flat 10% contingency is deliberate: real student events overrun, and a tool that budgets to the cent teaches orgs a bad habit.

Warn, then block

Three verdicts, because a binary yes/no is either too permissive or too rigid:

VerdictConditionBehaviour
OKRemaining after event ≥ 0Approve normally
TIGHTOverruns by up to 10% of allocationWarn loudly, still allow — orgs sometimes have good reasons
OVEROverruns by more than 10%Block approval; agent proposes ranked cuts

The design principle: the agent advises, the human decides. Suggested cuts are never auto-applied. An AI that silently deletes a line from a budget is an AI nobody trusts with a budget.

7. System Architecture — The Monolith

Why a monolith is correct here

  • One deploy target. Render free tier gives one always-on-ish web service. Splitting the bot into its own service doubles cold-start pain for no benefit.
  • Shared transactions. Confirming an event writes to events, reservations, and budgets. In one process that is one database transaction. Across services it is a distributed-transaction problem you do not have time to solve.
  • Feature modules give you the boundaries anyway. Each member works inside their own package with a defined interface. You get parallel work without network hops.

The bot runs inside the API process via FastAPI's lifespan, launched as an asyncio task. It shares the same connection pool, the same models, and the same feature services — a Discord command is a thin adapter over the same function the REST endpoint calls.

10. Supabase Setup

Create the project

  1. New project → pick the region nearest your venue → save the database password immediately, it is shown once.
  2. Wait for provisioning (~2 minutes).
  3. Settings → Database → Connection string. Copy the Session pooler URI, not the direct connection.
Critical gotcha. On the Supabase free plan the direct connection (db.<ref>.supabase.co:5432) is reachable over IPv6 only; the IPv4 add-on is a paid extra. Render does not give you IPv6 egress, so a direct connection string will fail with a connection error that looks like a firewall problem. The Shared Pooler (Supavisor) is IPv4 on every tier — use it. Session mode for a long-running API; transaction mode (port 6543) only if you later go serverless. Free tier also caps you at 500 MB storage, 500 MB RAM, and 200 pooler connections, all far beyond what this project needs.

Enable the extensions

Supabase SQL Editor → new query → run:

CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS pgcrypto;    -- gen_random_uuid()
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS btree_gist;  -- exclusion constraints

SELECT extname FROM pg_extension
WHERE extname IN ('pgcrypto','btree_gist');   -- expect 2 rows

Two databases, not one

Create a second free Supabase project called syncup-dev. Everyone develops against dev; only main deploys point at prod. Reseeding the demo database at hour 22 because someone was testing against it is a recoverable disaster you can simply avoid.

The connection string Supabase shows starts with postgresql://. SQLAlchemy's async engine needs postgresql+asyncpg://. Rewrite the prefix in config.py so nobody has to remember, and strip any ?sslmode= query parameter — asyncpg rejects it. Pass ssl via connect_args instead.

App entrypoint

# app/main.py
from contextlib import asynccontextmanager
from fastapi import FastAPI
from fastapi.middleware.cors import CORSMiddleware
from app.config import settings
from app.bot.client import start_bot, stop_bot
from app.jobs.scheduler import start_jobs
from app.features import scheduling, resources, finance

@asynccontextmanager
async def lifespan(app: FastAPI):
    bot_task = await start_bot()      # discord.py as an asyncio task
    start_jobs()                      # APScheduler
    yield
    await stop_bot(bot_task)

app = FastAPI(title="SyncUp API", lifespan=lifespan)

app.add_middleware(
    CORSMiddleware,
    allow_origins=settings.cors_origins,   # localhost + *.vercel.app + prod domain
    allow_credentials=True,
    allow_methods=["*"], allow_headers=["*"],
)

app.include_router(scheduling.router, prefix="/api/scheduling", tags=["scheduling"])
app.include_router(resources.router,  prefix="/api/resources",  tags=["resources"])
app.include_router(finance.router,    prefix="/api/finance",    tags=["finance"])

@app.get("/health")
async def health():
    return {"status": "ok"}

13. Microsoft Foundry Setup

Create a Foundry project, deploy a small chat model (gpt-4o-mini or equivalent), and copy the endpoint URL and key from the project's overview page.

Read the endpoint and model name from your own Foundry project page rather than hardcoding a URL from a tutorial — endpoint hostnames differ by resource and region. Also note that the older azure-ai-inference beta SDK is deprecated and being retired; the current guidance is to use the standard OpenAI SDK pointed at your Foundry endpoint, which is what the code below does. It also means you can swap to any OpenAI-compatible provider in one line if the free quota runs out mid-hackathon.
# app/core/llm.py
import json
from openai import AsyncOpenAI
from pydantic import BaseModel
from app.config import settings

client = AsyncOpenAI(
    base_url=settings.foundry_endpoint,   # from your Foundry project
    api_key=settings.foundry_api_key,
)

async def complete_json[T: BaseModel](
    system: str, user: str, schema: type[T], *, retries: int = 2
) -> T:
    """Call the model and validate against a Pydantic schema.
    Retries on malformed output, then raises. Never patches bad JSON."""
    for attempt in range(retries + 1):
        resp = await client.chat.completions.create(
            model=settings.foundry_model,
            messages=[
                {"role": "system", "content": system},
                {"role": "user",   "content": user},
            ],
            response_format={"type": "json_object"},
            temperature=0.2,        # low: we want consistency, not creativity
        )
        try:
            return schema.model_validate_json(resp.choices[0].message.content)
        except Exception:
            if attempt == retries:
                raise

Prompt discipline

  • Always request JSON against a Pydantic schema. Free-text parsing will break at hour 20.
  • Low temperature (0.1–0.3). You want the same answer twice on stage.
  • Inject real context — the org's inventory, its historical prices. Grounding beats prompt-engineering.
  • Log everything to ai_interactions: prompt, tool calls, latency, tokens. When a judge asks how you know the AI is right, you open the dashboard.
  • Cache aggressively during development. A dict keyed on the prompt hash saves your free quota and makes iteration instant.

14. Discord Bot Setup

  1. Discord Developer Portal → New Application → Bot → Reset Token, copy it.
  2. Enable Message Content Intent under Privileged Gateway Intents if you want @SyncUp mentions as well as slash commands.
  3. OAuth2 → URL Generator → scopes bot + applications.commands; permissions: Send Messages, Embed Links, Use Slash Commands, Read Message History.
  4. Open the generated URL and add the bot to your test server.
# app/bot/client.py
import asyncio, discord
from discord import app_commands
from app.config import settings

intents = discord.Intents.default()
intents.message_content = True
bot = discord.Client(intents=intents)
tree = app_commands.CommandTree(bot)

@bot.event
async def on_ready():
    # Guild-scoped sync is instant; global sync can take up to an hour.
    guild = discord.Object(id=settings.discord_guild_id)
    tree.copy_global_to(guild=guild)
    await tree.sync(guild=guild)
    print(f"SyncUp online as {bot.user}")

async def start_bot():
    return asyncio.create_task(bot.start(settings.discord_bot_token))

async def stop_bot(task):
    await bot.close()
    task.cancel()
Always sync commands to your test guild, never globally, during the hackathon. Global command propagation can take up to an hour — long enough to convince you the bot is broken when it is merely slow.

Interaction pattern: acknowledge immediately with await interaction.response.defer(thinking=True), then followup.send(...) once the agent returns. Discord kills an un-acknowledged interaction after 3 seconds, and an LLM round trip is slower than that.

Bind to $PORT — Render injects it and a hardcoded port fails the health check. Running migrations in the build command keeps schema and code in lockstep on every deploy.

The single biggest demo risk in this stack. A Render free web service spins down after 15 minutes without inbound traffic, and cold start takes roughly 30–60 seconds. If your service naps while you walk to the stage, your first /plan command times out in front of the judges.

Mitigations, in order:
1. Set up an external uptime pinger (UptimeRobot, cron-job.org, or a GitHub Action on a schedule) hitting /health every 10 minutes from the moment you first deploy.
2. Hit the endpoint yourself 2–3 minutes before you present, and confirm it responds.
3. Note that free services also have an ephemeral filesystem — never write uploads or a SQLite file to local disk; everything durable goes to Supabase.

Database → Supabase

Migrations run automatically via the Render build command. To apply one manually:

cd api
DATABASE_URL="<prod pooler url>" alembic upgrade head

Supabase's dashboard gives you a SQL editor and a table browser — useful for showing judges real rows, and for the 3am moment when you need to check what actually got written.

Deployment order at hour 0

  1. Supabase projects created, extensions enabled
  2. Repo scaffolded, /health returns 200 locally
  3. Render service live, /health returns 200 in production
  4. Vercel deployed, dashboard loads and calls /health successfully
  5. Uptime pinger configured

Do all five in the first two hours, while everything is trivial to debug. Teams that leave deployment to hour 20 lose the demo, not the build.
